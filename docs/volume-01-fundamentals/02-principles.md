# 1.2 Principles of Insurance

Insurance contracts are built on six core principles. They determine the validity of the contract, the amount payable, and the rights of the parties. Underwriters must apply them daily.

## The Six Core Principles

1. Insurable interest
2. Utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei)
3. Indemnity
4. Subrogation
5. Contribution
6. Proximate cause

---

## 1. Insurable Interest

**Insurable interest** exists when the insured has a financial or legal interest in the subject matter of insurance such that the insured would suffer economic or legal harm if the insured event occurred.

### Why It Matters

- Without insurable interest, a policy is a wagering contract and is void or unenforceable.
- It prevents people from insuring property or lives in which they have no stake, removing the temptation to cause loss for gain.

### When It Must Exist

| Type of Insurance | When Interest Must Exist |
|---|---|
| Property | At the time of loss (must also exist at inception in most jurisdictions) |
| Life | At the time the policy is issued (for the person taking out the policy) |
| Liability | At the time the loss occurs |
| Marine | At the time of loss (and generally also at inception) |

### Examples

- A homeowner has an insurable interest in their home.
- A lender has an insurable interest in mortgaged property to the extent of the loan.
- A business has an insurable interest in its inventory, buildings, and key employees.
- A spouse has an insurable interest in the life of the other spouse.

---

## 2. Utmost Good Faith

**Utmost good faith** (uberrimae fidei) requires both parties to a contract of insurance to act honestly and to disclose every **material fact** — a fact that would influence a prudent insurer in deciding whether to accept a risk and on what terms.

### The Duty of Disclosure

- The **applicant/insured** must disclose material facts known to them that are not known or deemed known by the insurer.
- The duty applies at proposal, on renewal, and during the policy period when facts change materially.
- The **insurer** must also act fairly, e.g., by explaining the contract and handling claims promptly.

### Consequences of Non-Disclosure / Misrepresentation

| Situation | Effect |
|---|---|
| Fraudulent non-disclosure or misrepresentation | Policy is void; the insurer may keep the premium and decline all claims |
| Innocent or negligent non-disclosure | Policy may be avoided; in some jurisdictions (e.g., UK under the Insurance Act 2015) remedies are proportionate — fair claim handling, or recission with return of premium |
| Misrepresentation that is not material | No remedy; the policy stands |

### Modern Statutory Overlay

- **UK:** The Insurance Act 2015 replaced the strict rule for business insurance with a duty of **fair presentation** — the insured must disclose material circumstances or provide sufficient information to put a prudent insurer on notice. The Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012 applies to consumers.
- **US:** States vary. Many impose a duty of good faith and fair dealing, and some limit avoidance to material misrepresentations that contribute to the loss or were made fraudulently.

---

## 3. Indemnity

**Indemnity** means the insured is restored, so far as money can do it, to the financial position they occupied immediately before the loss — no better and no worse.

### How Indemnity Is Applied

| Basis | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Actual Cash Value (ACV)** | Replacement cost less depreciation |
| **Replacement Cost (RC / RCV)** | Cost to repair or replace with like kind and quality, without deduction for depreciation (subject to policy conditions) |
| **Agreed value** | A stated value agreed at inception (e.g., for fine art, marine hull of a specified class) |
| **Market value** | The price an asset would fetch in the market (sometimes used for land/buildings) |

### Limits on Indemnity

- **Limit of liability** — the policy cap.
- **Deductible** — the insured's share.
- **Condition of average / coinsurance** — if the insured under-insures, payment is proportionately reduced (common in UK commercial fire policies: the "average" or "underinsurance" clause).
- **Salvage** — the insurer may take damaged property as salvage in total-loss settlements.

### Exceptions to Indemnity

- **Life and personal accident** are not strictly contracts of indemnity because a human life cannot be valued. The sum assured is fixed in the policy.
- **Valued policies** (e.g., agreed value on fine art) pay the agreed amount on a total loss.
- Some jurisdictions (e.g., certain US states) have valued policy laws for total property losses.

---

## 4. Subrogation

**Subrogation** gives the insurer, after paying a claim, the right to **step into the insured's shoes** and recover from a third party who caused or is responsible for the loss.

### Why Subrogation Exists

- To prevent the insured from **double recovery** (from both the insurer and the wrongdoer).
- To ensure the **party at fault** ultimately bears the cost of the loss.
- To help keep premiums down by returning recovered funds to the insurer.

### How It Works

1. The insurer pays the insured's covered claim.
2. The insurer acquires the insured's rights of recovery against the third party.
3. The insurer pursues recovery (often through the insured's cooperation).
4. Recoveries reduce the loss cost of the policy.

### The Insured's Duties

- Do **not** sign releases or waivers that destroy the rights of recovery.
- Cooperate with the insurer's recovery efforts.
- Take reasonable steps to preserve evidence and legal rights.

---

## 5. Contribution

**Contribution** applies when **two or more policies** cover the same loss. It allows the insurers to **share the loss in proportion** to their respective limits, so that the insured does not recover more than the amount of the loss (indemnity is preserved) and no insurer bears more than its fair share.

### Conditions for Contribution

- The policies cover the same subject matter.
- They cover the same peril or risk.
- They insure the same interest of the same insured.
- The loss is a covered loss under both policies.

### How Contribution Is Calculated

The most common basis is **rateable proportion**:

> Each insurer pays: (its own limit ÷ total limits of all contributing policies) × the loss

### "Other Insurance" Clauses

Policies usually contain an **Other Insurance** condition that determines how multiple policies respond:

| Clause Type | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Escape / excess** | This policy applies only after other insurance is exhausted |
| **Pro rata** | Shares the loss proportionately with other insurance |
| **Primary / excess** | Defines which policy pays first |
| **Contribution (rateable)** | Each policy contributes by share of limits |

---

## 6. Proximate Cause

**Proximate cause** is the **dominant and effective cause** of a loss — the cause that sets in motion a chain of events leading to the loss, without which the loss would not have occurred. Coverage under a policy depends on whether the proximate cause is a **covered peril**.

### Why It Matters

- A policy covers specified perils (fire, theft, etc.) or, under open perils, all perils not excluded.
- If the proximate cause is a covered peril, the loss is covered even if later events in the chain are not.
- If the proximate cause is excluded (e.g., flood, earthquake, wear and tear), the loss is not covered, subject to **concurrent causation** rules.

### Worked Examples

| Chain of Events | Proximate Cause | Covered? |
|---|---|---|
| Fire breaks out → explosion → damage | Fire | Covered under fire policy |
| Flood rises → building collapses → contents damaged | Flood | Excluded if flood is excluded |
| Earthquake → fire → damage | Depends on jurisdiction; in many places fire following earthquake is covered separately, in others earthquake is the proximate cause and is excluded |
| Wear and tear → roof leaks → contents damaged | Wear and tear (excluded) | Not covered |

### Concurrent Causation

When **two or more causes** act together to produce a loss — one covered and one excluded — the modern approach is generally:

- If a covered cause is a **direct, independent, and proximate** cause of the loss, coverage applies (the **"efficient proximate cause"** doctrine used in many US jurisdictions).
- The 2005 US case *State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. v. Von Der Lieth* illustrates the points where courts allocate between covered and excluded causes.

### Anti-Concurrent Causation (ACC) Clauses

Many modern property policies include **anti-concurrent causation** clauses stating that if an excluded peril contributes to the loss, no coverage applies — even where a covered peril also contributed. These clauses are litigated in many jurisdictions; their validity varies by state and by whether a covered peril is an independent cause of damage.

---

## Application of the Principles

| Principle | Applies When | Typical Use in Underwriting |
|---|---|---|
| Insurable interest | Policy inception (life) / loss (property, liability) | Verifying the applicant's relationship to the subject matter |
| Utmost good faith | At proposal and throughout | Asking material questions; reviewing disclosures and warranties |
| Indemnity | At settlement | Choosing valuation basis (ACV vs. RC); applying average |
| Subrogation | After payment | Pursuing third-party recovery |
| Contribution | Multiple policies | Coordinating "other insurance" clauses |
| Proximate cause | At claim determination | Determining whether a loss is covered or excluded |

---

## Summary

- The six principles define the legal and moral framework of insurance.
- They protect the **pool** (preventing fraud and double recovery) and keep premiums fair.
- Underwriters rely on them when evaluating risk, drafting terms, and handling the consequences of claims.
